Questions

What is Competitive Programming?

As the name might imply, Competitive Programming is a sport, but not a physical sport. Instead of building projects and creating apps, you are given a list of problems and a time limit, and you try to solve as many problems as you can. Difficulties can range from easy to extremely difficult, but all problems require some level of algorithmic and logical thinking.

Key Parts of a Contest

  • The Problems: for each problem, you are given some kind of scenario (like a word problem in math), then you are given inputs, and the expected output for each input.
  • The Constraints: Every problem has constraints, or the maximum and minimum the input will take. This is crucial because it dictates how efficient your code must be. If your code is too slow, then it will not past the test cases.
  • Evaluation: When you submit your code, an automated grading system (called an online judge) will test your code on various inputs to see if your outputs are correct and fit within the time and memory limits. In a live contest, you are usually not able to see some of the test cases to ensure that you can't cheat. In a practice settings (casually solving problems), you are usually able to see all test cases.
  • Scoring: Usually determined by how many problems you are solved, and how fast you solved each problem compared to other people. Depending on which platform you take the contest on, you may receive a penalty for wrong submissions (e.g. 10 minute penalty, -50 score, etc).

Why was Algostep created?

If you did not know yet, Algostep was created by just one person (Me). I'm not some kind of programming wizard who was recruited by Google or some other big tech company, if that's what you expected the creator of Algostep to be like. In fact, I'm still struggling to solve problems to this day. So, some people who are using this website might actually be even better than I am at Competitive Programming.

Every time you get stuck on a problem, you usually go to the editorial for some help, which is completely reasonable. But I found often times that some editorials are written by some of the best competitive programmers, meaning they assume you know a lot of topics, when, in reality, you don't. As a result, the editorial might sound really abstract and confusing; some are just a few sentences long only (most notoriously, Codeforces). This kills motivation extremely fast, and it did for me.

I knew that some people were quitting Competitive Programming because of these poorly-written editorials, so I built Algostep to aim to solve this problem: every problem will get well-crafted hints, and beginner-friendly editorials. The goal is to make every solution to a problem clear and straightforward, never assuming people know difficult or abstract topics.

How does Algostep work?

Algostep's primary goal is to significantly assist programmers who are on their Competitive Programming journey. Whether you are completely new or have experience, Algostep is meant to provide insights that will help.

  • What specific features does Algostep have?
    • A skill-tree containing various topics ranging from Beginner to Intermediate level
    • Many Competitive Programming problems taken from various Competitive Programming websites
    • A leveling system that locks certain topics and problems behind progression
    • Profile statistics (e.g. number of problems solved, problems in progress, etc.)
  • How do the problems work?
    • Problems are taken from various websites (e.g. LeetCode, Codeforces, USACO, etc.)
    • Each problem has the problem statement, 2-3 Hints, an Editorial, and coded solutions
    • Able to change the status of a problem (Not Solved, In Progress, Completed) and star a problem
    • Solving a problem gives XP (experience points) based on the difficulty of the problem
    • Problems are categorized as Easy, Medium, or Hard
    • Traffic light scheme of Green, Yellow, and Red for the difficulties
  • How do you progress in Algostep?
    • There are three User Levels: New, Beginner, and Intermediate
    • Within each User Level are 3 Mini Levels
    • You progress through each level by obtaining XP
    • The harder the problem, the more XP you gain when solving it
    • The XP required for each level increases as you level up more

How do you get good at Competitive Programming?

Pretty generic question, but nonetheless an important one. Simple Answer: Solve a lot of problems. However, I'm sure you've heard this answer at least a thousand times, so let's break it down.

  • Discipline > Motivation.
    • It's much better to solve 1 problem a day for a week then solve 15 problems in one day for 1 month
    • You don't go on a 5-hour problem-solving sprint every day in order to "get good"
    • Too much practice in a day leads to quick burnout
    • Form a habit of sitting down and trying to solve a problem daily
    • Competitive Programming requires immense dedication; do not expect to become a master within a week or even a couple of months
  • The Comfort Zone is not your friend
    • Solving problems that you already know you can easily solve is a waste of time
    • Think about it: if you can solve a problem easily, then you don't learn anything out of it
    • Always solve problems that are slightly out of your comfort zone
    • Solving 1 hard problem in 3 hours is better than solving 10 easy problems in the same amount of time
    • That said, do not try to solve a problem that is obviously way too hard (leads to burnout)
  • Know when to raise the white flag
    • Knowing when to give up and look at the solution is extremely crucial
    • There are two general methods: look at the solution as soon as you're stuck, or spend 30-45 minutes trying different methods first
    • For new competitive programmers, it's recommended to look at the solution immediately, since you don't know any concepts yet; staring at a problem isn't a good thing
    • For more experienced people, you should spend around 30-45 minutes of trying before giving up, as doing this builds crucial problem-solving intuition
  • Use an editorial wisely
    • If available, look at the hints first before the full solution
    • Otherwise, try to read only the first couple of sentences of the solution
    • If you find something new, try to implement it
    • Sometimes, you might have to look at the full solution, which is completely fine
    • Never memorize the code or how to do the problem; learn how to recognize the pattern
    • Don't copy and paste the code over; manually type it to build a sense of familiarity
    • If you can solve the problem 2-3 days later, then you've learned something
  • Have fun.
    • Not having fun while solving problems will lead to burnout fast
    • You don't necessarily have to have fun 24/7; burnout can happen even when you have fun
    • I understand if you're doing something like USACO only for college applications, but without passion, it's going to be hard to get far
    • Even if your parents are forcing you to do programming, passion is still extremely important
    • If you are burnt out, then take a break; doing more will only exacerbate burnout

One final note: Artificial Intelligence (AI) will be your best friend on your Competitive Programming journey. AI is very good at teaching you programming concepts, especially beginner-level ones. Asking ChatGPT or Gemini can help you understand the problem and the solution better, but it is important that you never blindly copy-and-paste their code, as you will not learn anything from that. Use AI as a tool, not a cheating device.

What's the ideal roadmap of progression?

(Up to USACO Bronze)

There isn't a single, strict roadmap that you should follow for Competitive Programming. So, the one that I will provide is the one that I personally went through. Please note that this is not strictly linear; you will often jump back and forth between different stages. Also, this is just a rough roadmap, so I don't go too much in detail for the different stages, since it entirely depends on your preferences.

  • Beginner Stages
    • Learn a programming language and get very comfortable with its syntax
    • Language depends on your preference, but C++, Python, or Java are good options
    • Go to Codewars to practice beginner-friendly problems to train logical thinking and get comfortable with syntax.
    • If stuck, you can ask AI for help (make sure to actually learn what it explains)
    • This stage is very important; you need to lock down the fundamentals before attempting harder problems
  • Elementary Stages
    • Start learning Data Structures and Algorithms (DSA)
    • Go to LeetCode to practice problems covering DSA
    • Follow NeetCode's Roadmap to learn DSA
    • NeetCode has excellent solutions and insights to LeetCode problems
    • Core topics to learn: Arrays & Hashing, Two Pointers, Stack, Binary Search, Sliding Window
    • Learn about Time Complexity and how to use problem constraints
    • Imporant: Never do random problems, always practice problems in certain topics you are learning
  • Intermediate Stages
    • Go to Codeforces to solve harder problems that are more focused on Competitive Programming
    • Filter problems rated from 800 to 900 (eventually, you can up the difficulty)
    • Try to participate in some contests (Div. 4 or Div. 3)
    • Stay consistent in problem solving, and be prepared to be demotivated at times
    • Doing Codewars and LeetCode is still good
  • Advanced Stages
    • By now, you can most likely do some USACO Bronze problems
    • Go to USACO Guide to learn more concepts for USACO
    • Read through and do the starred problems for the Bronze section
    • Do not feel obligated to solve the Hard or Very Hard problems; you can solve them later
    • Important: Solve a lot of problems (a lot)
    • Keep a notebook log of any problems that you particularly struggled with
    • Review your notebook every couple of days or weeks
